How Equal Temperament Ruined Harmony (and Why You Should Care)
Ross W. Duffin
W. W. Norton, 2007
ISBN 9780393062274 (hardcover); 9780393334203 (paperback)

What the book is doing

Duffin argues against treating twelve-tone equal temperament (12-TET) as the uniquely natural or historically universal way to tune Western music. In 12-TET the octave has frequency ratio 2:1 and is divided into twelve exactly equal logarithmic steps. Each semitone therefore multiplies frequency by 2^(1/12).

That gives enormous practical convenience: one fixed-pitch keyboard can play in every key, and enharmonic spellings such as F-sharp and G-flat can be mapped to one key. The cost is that most intervals are slightly displaced from simple whole-number frequency ratios. Equal temperament distributes tuning error rather than eliminating it.

Duffin's historical argument is that many composers and performers used unequal temperaments long after equal temperament was mathematically available. Older temperaments gave different keys and intervals measurably different acoustical characters. A performance in modern equal temperament can therefore erase distinctions that were part of the historical sound-world of the music.

The useful computational point

This book belongs in a representation that distinguishes exact ratios, continuous pitch values, temperament rules, and note spelling.

A convenient neutral representation is logarithmic pitch:

    pitch = log2(frequency / reference_frequency)

An octave is then exactly 1.0 and a 12-TET semitone is exactly 1/12 octave. Cents are simply 1200 times this octave coordinate.

But ratio information should not be thrown away. A pure perfect fifth is 3/2. A pure major third is 5/4. Their logarithmic sizes can be compared directly with equal-tempered approximations.

For example:

    pure fifth          = log2(3/2) octaves
    12-TET fifth        = 7/12 octave

    pure major third    = log2(5/4) octaves
    12-TET major third  = 4/12 octave

The difference is a small continuous quantity: exactly the sort of 'comma-sized' discrepancy Duffin cares about.

F-sharp versus G-flat

In 12-TET, F-sharp and G-flat normally have identical frequency. In unequal temperament or expressive intonation they need not. Historical tuning systems can assign different sizes to different semitones, so the spelling can carry information about intended intervallic direction and sounding pitch.

This suggests keeping a note as more than a single integer 0..11. At minimum preserve:

- letter name;
- accidental;
- octave/register when relevant;
- temperament;
- exact ratio when one is known or intended;
- realized frequency/log-frequency as a floating value.

Then enharmonic equivalence is a derived relation, not an axiom.

Ratios and floats

The exact musical relation and its physical realization are different things. Ratios are excellent for representing ideal just intervals and chains of tuning relations. Floating-point/logarithmic values are excellent for comparing actual pitch locations, cents, beating tendencies, measurements, and temperament mappings.

So a useful model is not 'ratio OR float' but:

    symbolic interval / exact ratio
              -> temperament or intonation rule
              -> continuous sounding pitch

The residual between two realizations can then be measured in cents or octaves without losing the exact symbolic relation that generated them.

Connection to Tymoczko

Duffin asks: where, acoustically, is the note?
Tymoczko asks: where, geometrically, is the chord or voice-leading object after we choose which distinctions to identify?

These should be separate layers. A chord-space point can retain a temperament-independent symbolic identity while a realization layer maps each voice to a physical frequency. This makes it possible to compare the same abstract voice leading in equal temperament, meantone, well temperament, or just intonation.
